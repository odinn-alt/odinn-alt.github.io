Built from scratch The outbound sales engineArchitected end to end, from the DNS records to the messaging. Zero-touch from first email to created deal

Apollo · Zoho CRM + Flow · Deluge · Claude AI · O365 · GlockApps · verification

This one is mine from the ground up. I architected the company's entire outbound machine, from the plumbing to the messaging, and it now identifies prospects, runs personalized sequences, reads its own replies, and converts interested leads into CRM deals with zero human triage. It has produced real traction: brand awareness, engaged pipeline, and new accounts.

The plumbing came first, because cold email dies at the infrastructure layer. All volume sends from dedicated sending subdomains I set up specifically to protect the corporate domain's reputation. Every sending domain carries SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records I configured and monitor. New mailboxes go through a structured warming process before they ever carry campaign volume. GlockApps seed accounts verify actual inbox placement per provider, and every address passes a bounce checker before its first send, because bounces are the fastest way to burn a sender score.

The machine itself is a graduation system. Apollo runs our cold outreach through two sequences, fed by prospect lists matched to our ideal customer profile plus leads I research by hand in LinkedIn Sales Navigator for higher-value targets. Every send, open, click, and reply feeds a lead score, and I set the benchmarks: prospects who engage and cross them graduate out of cold outreach into Zoho journeys. Graduates land first in the evergreen series, a standing drip of genuinely useful cross-border and warehousing education that warms them without pitching them. From there they move to the nurture newsletter, our recurring value-first email of industry updates and company news, the long-term relationship channel where qualified contacts live until they are ready to talk. Warm leads skip the cold machine entirely: direct inquiries, trade show contacts, and referrals are already qualified, so they go straight into the newsletter.

Replies forward through a chain into a backend inbox that fires a Zoho Flow, where Claude AI reads each reply, strips the quoted thread so only the lead's own words count, and classifies intent. An interested reply becomes a Contact, Account, and Deal within the minute, assigned to me, because the last mile is not automated: I run the outreach conversations, the discovery calls, and I close the deals. The system has produced a steady stream of direct inquiries and new accounts. And all of it complies with anti-spam law: CAN-SPAM in the U.S. and CASL for Canadian recipients, with identification on every send and unsubscribes honored permanently.

  • Dedicated sending subdomains, SPF/DKIM/DMARC per domain, GlockApps placement seeds, structured mailbox warming, bounce verification before any first send
  • Two Apollo cold sequences fed by ICP-matched lists plus manual LinkedIn Sales Navigator prospecting
  • Lead scoring with graduation benchmarks I set: engaged leads promote from cold into Zoho journeys
  • Evergreen series (educational warming drip) → nurture newsletter (recurring value-first relationship channel)
  • Warm leads from inquiries, trade shows, and referrals enter the newsletter directly, already qualified
  • AI reply classification (Claude) with quoted-thread stripping; interested replies auto-convert to Contact + Account + Deal
  • I run the human last mile: outreach conversations, discovery calls, and closing
  • CAN-SPAM and CASL compliant: identification on every send, permanent unsubscribe suppression

Built from scratch The Sales TrackerMy most cumulative build: a full CRM, mailbox intelligence engine, analytics suite, meeting recorder, and pricing hub in one private app

Next.js · Prisma · SQLite · Whisper · Claude / Ollama switchable · PWA

This is the most important thing I've built at NEXgistics, and the most cumulative: it grew feature by feature until it became the platform the whole commercial operation runs on. At its core sits a mailbox intelligence engine. Every email in the connected mailbox is pulled in, grouped into threads, and classified as sales-relevant or not, filtered against the company domain, known teammates, self-email aliases, and noise patterns I've tuned over months. Each sales thread carries a stage, cold through won, a category, and detected signals: meetings, term sheets sent or signed, reply timing. That classification layer is the single source of truth every other feature reads, dashboard, pipeline, analytics, and the Zoho bridge alike, so one filter fix ripples correctly through everything.

As a daily driver it opens on a briefing: how many conversations are waiting on me, follow-ups due, overdue next steps, and a prioritized action queue with five buckets, new inquiries, follow up, needs your reply, active, and stale, so nothing sits unanswered. Threads get AI-drafted replies shaped by a writing-style learner trained on my own sent mail, greeting, sign-off, tone, and length, not a generic template. A background LiveSync checks for new mail every minute with browser notifications, global search runs command-palette style across companies, threads, and meetings, and a review queue triages sales-adjacent mail before it can pollute the pipeline: Keep makes it a real lead, Dismiss hides it forever without ever deleting it.

The CRM layer holds the full client book with AI-written company descriptions, per company profiles with complete activity timelines, and a kanban pipeline where term sheets detected in mail advance a company's stage automatically. Analytics run quarter over quarter with deltas: engaged leads, meetings, term sheet conversion and days to sign, reply rates, loss reasons, plus board-ready PDF and Excel exports. Term Sheet Intelligence goes further, analyzing my actual sent term sheets to find what correlates with getting signed, rates, deal size, meeting-first versus not, so the standard template is built from what converts, and the pricing hub proposes new term sheets anchored to that same history, generating the Word document and PDF in our real template.

Around the edges it keeps growing arms. An outbound view mirrors the Apollo engine live and cross-checks CRM overlap so nobody cold-emails an account we're already talking to. The Zoho bridge matches every engaged lead to a real Zoho record by trying every known contact email, stages tags and follow-up tasks locally, and writes to Zoho only on one confirmed click, never automatically. The meetings library replaced a paid transcription tool: I record from my phone as a PWA, audio transcribes locally with Whisper on the office PC so recordings never leave the building, speaker-labeled and summarized. A business card scanner reads trade show cards with Claude vision and cross-checks them against the mailbox. All of it runs as a private local server, Next.js, Prisma, and SQLite, reachable from my devices over an encrypted private network, installable as a PWA, with the AI provider switchable between cloud Claude, free local Ollama, or off entirely, and a standing security document because mailbox data deserves that respect.

Built from scratch Content StudioThe content factory: four voices, three formats, real citations, real photos, judged drafts, and cost engineering down to the cent

Next.js 16 · React 19 · Prisma/SQLite · Anthropic API · RSS research · PWA-style local app

Content Studio is as important to the company as the Sales Tracker: it streamlined and automated our entire content operation, LinkedIn, newsletter, and blog, and it is the engine behind the three accounts I grew from zero. Everything generates from a two-axis matrix: four voices by three formats. The voices, company, academic, founder, and my own, are driven by live Markdown voice guides, so editing a guide changes output immediately. The academic voice is special: fully objective third-party analysis that never names or promotes the company, evidence-led and cited. The formats know their rules too: LinkedIn posts run hook-first under the character limit with proper hashtag discipline, the newsletter has its own disarming register, and blogs come out 700 to 1,300 words with a thesis H1, declarative H2s, a meta description, and an MLA works cited list.

The prompt system is layered like a compliance stack: accuracy rules, then punctuation rules, then the house style base layer, then the voice overlay, then format rules. Accuracy rules forbid invented statistics, dates, or citations outright. Punctuation rules ban the telltale AI patterns. And source material is explicitly treated as untrusted data, so a prompt injection buried in a scraped article cannot redirect the model. Generation runs three distinct angles in parallel, an operational-reality take, a contrarian expert take, and a process story, so variants read genuinely different. Every draft then faces a judge pass against voice, format, accuracy, and structure rules, with a conditional rewrite only when the judge finds fault, which avoids paying to re-type clean drafts. Each variant surfaces the judge's notes, verified facts, and claims-to-verify before anything gets posted.

Citations are real. Academic drafts pull actual reporting from a reputable-source allowlist, Reuters, AP, Bloomberg, and the trade press, via RSS and topic-scoped news search at zero API cost, relevance-gated so off-topic articles can't sneak in. Paid web search is the last resort, capped and locked to the cheap model. Every source is captured with title, author, outlet, date, and live URL: LinkedIn gets a ready-to-paste MLA first comment, blogs get an in-body works cited list. The Discover page scans trade feeds and proposes ranked, voice-matched topics that persist to the database, one click away from a pre-filled draft. The Library logs every generation automatically with variants, sources, cost, and graphic prompts, then tracks the posting workflow: impressions, reactions, and comments logged over time, per-account engagement insights, and a "more like this" respin for winners.

The visual side is handled too. A gallery of 247 real warehouse photos, vision-tagged for pennies, pairs relevant photography to each post through free keyword matching, and a graphic kit builds a ready-to-paste art-direction prompt per voice and format, brand colors and composition rules included, with the whole bundle downloadable as one ZIP. Costs are engineered, not hoped for: prompt caching with a pre-warm call cuts the big shared prompt to a tenth of the price for parallel calls, models are gated per task, and every call lands in the shared usage ledger the Sales Tracker rolls up. The stack is Next.js and Prisma over SQLite, provider-switchable with a local Ollama fallback, with API middleware guarding against DNS rebinding and CSRF, a one-click launcher that sets up a fresh machine, and source backups to the operations hub after every session.

Autonomous trading research TraderAn autonomous paper-trading analyst, a pre-registered research lab, and an execution bridge that refuses unsafe plans

Python · pandas · SQLite · Alpaca API · custom backtest engine · full web dashboard

Trader is the longest-running build I have, still in active development, and it is three systems in one. The first is the autonomous analyst. During market hours it scans roughly 800 symbols, screens them through pattern signals and risk gates, enters positions with protective stops set before the fill confirms, pyramids into winners, manages exits with cooldowns, and switches into research mode after the close. Every decision lands in a cumulative ledger, hourly trading reports and a daily health report push to my phone, and the whole thing runs around the clock as supervised services behind a full dashboard: a P/L cockpit, portfolio and charts, market intelligence pages, the strategy arena, and the bridge.

The second system exists because of an uncomfortable question: how do I know any of this actually works? The research lab treats a trading strategy like a scientific claim. Acceptance criteria are pre-registered before any results exist: minimum growth, risk-adjusted return, maximum drawdown, and beating a passive benchmark after realistic costs. A strategy develops against a decade of market history, and if it passes, it gets exactly one attempt at a held-out window of years it has never seen. The engine defends itself against the classic backtesting sins with a self-test suite that runs before any result is trusted, including a look-ahead canary that plants future data where a buggy engine would read it. Most of my strategies failed this gauntlet. Two diversified ensembles passed the full protocol, including the one-shot held-out test, and the champion runs the paper account today.

Candidates and survivors alike compete in the arena: fourteen strategy sleeves with $25,000 of paper money each, recomputed deterministically every session and marked to live quotes against an S&P 500 benchmark. As I write this, exactly one of the fourteen beats SPY, and the dashboard says so in large letters, because the point of the arena is truth, not comfort. Behind it sits the cohort research: more than a thousand journaled trading decisions analyzed for where the edge actually lives, with pre-registered A/B experiments, like the current test on stop width, deciding what changes next.

The third system is the execution bridge, which turns the champion's frozen strategy into real order plans on the brokerage API. It runs a guard chain before anything executes: books reconciled against the broker, drift within tolerance, every order sizable and safe. If a guard fails, the bridge refuses the entire plan and tells me why, and it has refused before. Layered circuit breakers sit outside the validated drawdown envelope, fill quality is tracked against the modeled slippage, and every event is journaled. All of it is paper money by deliberate policy. Real capital stays off until the system passes a dedicated security review, and the fact that it hasn't yet is a feature of the process, not a gap.

Study platform CBLE Prep628 real exam questions, a faithful replica of the real testing software, and a body of federal law that keeps itself current

Python · FastAPI · React PWA · spaced repetition · PDF ingestion · local AI

I'm preparing for the U.S. Customs Broker License Exam, a federal exam with roughly a 10 to 15 percent pass rate, and the commercial study tools weren't good enough. So I built the platform I wanted, and I study on it every day, on my desktop and installed on my phone. The question bank is 628 real questions from eight past exams, extracted and cleaned from the official CBP booklets, with the questions CBP itself voided correctly excluded and CBP's own answer-key typos preserved faithfully rather than silently corrected. Study is scheduled by spaced repetition with lapse cooldowns, so the cards I struggle with come back often but never loop back-to-back, and targeted practice drills go straight at my mistakes and weakest topics.

The centerpiece is the exam simulator, and I built it after researching how the real computer-based test actually behaves, down to its annoyances. References open on top of your question, the search highlights but will not jump you to the next match, and there is no copy-paste, so practicing here builds the exact muscle the real exam demands. Sessions run at the real pace of about three and a half minutes per question, draw questions by stratified sampling that mirrors the real exam's topic blueprint, support flagging for review, and grade like the real thing. When a session ends, the result screen replays every question with all five choices, what I picked, what was right, and an explanation on demand from a locally hosted model.

The law inside the platform keeps itself current, because studying stale regulation is worse than not studying. On every server start it checks the Code of Federal Regulations against the government's official source and the tariff schedule against the current revision, updating itself when Washington changes something. Nearly every question deep-links to the exact regulation section that answers it, so a wrong answer becomes a reading assignment instead of a mystery. And the platform tracks the whole broker lifecycle past the exam: a brush-up mode of essentials for after passing, continuing education credits, and the countdown to the triennial status report every licensed broker owes CBP.

  • 628 real past-exam questions across eight CBP exams, voided items excluded, key errata preserved
  • Exam simulator with real pacing, stratified topic sampling, flagging, and honest grading
  • Fidelity details researched from the real testing platform: references cover the question, search highlights without jumping, no copy-paste
  • Result replay of every question with all choices, plus on-demand explanations from a local model
  • Spaced repetition with lapse cooldowns; drills targeting mistakes and weak topics
  • 19 CFR checked against the official source on every start; HTSUS tracked by revision
  • CBP guidance documents held in-app with visible freshness dates and a manual update check
  • Citation deep links from questions to the exact regulation section
  • License lifecycle: brush-up essentials, continuing education credits, triennial report countdown

AI assistant EVEA private AI ecosystem on my own hardware: chat, calendar, briefs, notifications, and a design language built from scratch

Python · FastAPI · React PWA · local LLMs · service workers · canvas

EVE is the personal project I have poured the most into, alongside Trader, and it is still in active development because an assistant is never finished. It is entirely separate from my work: nothing in EVE touches company systems or company data. It is a personal system for a personal life, and one of the main reasons I built it is privacy. I wanted my calendar, my conversations, and my daily life to live on my own machine instead of accumulating in some large corporation's cloud. That conviction drove the architecture. EVE started as a chat interface and grew into a private ecosystem running entirely on hardware I own: threaded conversations with persistent memory, a Today page that works like a cockpit for my day, a calendar that refuses to let me miss things, daily market briefs, and the notification system that ties all of it to my phone. Routine requests are answered by language models running locally, which costs nothing and keeps my data home; only harder requests escalate to a cloud model, and I decide where that line sits. Its memory is plain files I can open and edit, which is the same design position: an assistant should be inspectable by, and answerable to, the person it works for.

The Today page is the part I actually live in. A greeting and live clock, a system rail of status chips reporting on the rest of the ecosystem at a glance, whether Trader is active, how many pushes went out today, when the next reminder fires, whether today's brief is ready, then what's next on the calendar with a live countdown, action items I can check off inline, a seven-day radar of the week ahead, and a command row where I can ask EVE anything or add an event in natural language. Every afternoon EVE writes a structured eight-section market brief, and the calendar escalates: when an event is imminent, it re-pushes every minute until I acknowledge it, with hard caps so it can never nag forever. Missing a meeting because a phone buzzed once quietly is a solved problem in my life.

The notification system is its own piece of engineering. One gateway routes roughly thirty distinct notification kinds, calendar alarms, market moves, trade entries and exits, risk alerts, brief deliveries, job matches, to the right app on my phone as web push. Apple does not let a web app choose notification sounds in the background, every push plays the same tone, so differentiation had to move to the eye: each kind carries its own visual identity on the lock screen, recognizable at a glance. And the interface itself is a design system I built from scratch, called Mission Control: design tokens, a hand-drawn set of about forty five icons, view transitions, and a canvas-drawn orb identity that breathes while idle at zero paint cost and swirls while EVE is thinking. There is also an image-generation surface in progress, backed by local models, because the ecosystem keeps growing.

  • Threaded chat with persistent, plain-file memory I can open and audit
  • Hybrid model routing: local models first for privacy and cost, cloud escalation for quality
  • Today cockpit: system rail, next-up countdown, inline action items, week radar, natural-language quick add
  • Daily eight-section market brief, generated on schedule and pushed to my phone
  • Calendar nag-until-acknowledged: re-pushes every minute when imminent, with hard safety caps
  • Unified gateway routing ~30 notification kinds, each visually distinct on the lock screen
  • Installed as a phone app with a service-worker push pipeline over a private network
  • Mission Control design system: tokens, ~45 hand-built icons, view transitions, the canvas orb
  • Supervised services with watchdog auto-restart; image generation surface in development
